Six years ago I started my first ML job. If I could hand a one-page note back
to that version of myself, here is what it would say. Not all of it is about
ML, because most of what matters in this job turns out not to be.

## Ship something real, early

The fastest way to learn is to have your work used by people who do not care
about your feelings. Every model I shipped taught me more than every model I
polished. Ship small, ship often, and let production be the teacher.

## The data is the project

I thought I would spend my career in architectures and losses. I have spent
most of it in pipelines, labels, backfills, and arguing about what a feature
means. Accept this early: ML engineering is mostly data engineering with a
model at the end. The people who embrace that become better engineers; the
people who resent it stay frustrated.

## Learn the business language

"ARPU", "conversion", "net revenue": these words opened more doors for me
than any framework. When you can connect a model change to a business number,
you stop being someone who trains models and become someone who moves
metrics. Managers listen to the second person.

## Write things down

I started a blog years later than I should have. Every project left lessons
that I thought I would remember and then did not. Write the retrospective,
the postmortem, the note-to-self. Writing forces clarity, and clarity is
scarce.

## Boring beats clever

The production systems that last are the boring ones: idempotent pipelines,
evaluation sets, rollbacks, monitoring. The clever ideas have a short half
life. Optimise for maintainable, and the cleverness will find you anyway.

## Build the public habit early

Co-organizing a hackathon, giving talks, writing posts: none of this felt
important at the time, and all of it compounded. People find you through
what you make visible. Start before you feel ready.

## Protect your energy for the long game

This field moves fast and it is easy to burn out chasing it. The people who
are still in it after a decade are not the ones who knew the most at year
one. They are the ones who kept shipping, kept learning, and kept going.

## Find your people

The single highest-leverage thing I did was not a model or a course. It was
surrounding myself with people slightly ahead of me. A good team teaches you
more in six months than any curriculum, and a bad team costs you years of
momentum. When you choose where to work, look less at the tech stack and
more at the people you would learn from.

The same goes for community: the hackathon I co-organized, the talks I gave,
the conversations after them. Careers are built in networks, not in
notebooks. Be generous, be visible, and the field gives back more than it
takes.

If you are at year one of your ML career: the road is long, the boring parts
are the real work, and it gets better as you get better.
