Ask people what an ML engineer needs to know and they will say PyTorch,
transformers, maybe some math. Almost nobody says SQL, and almost everyone
who has shipped a model disagrees with that list.

Most of my working hours have been spent in front of a SQL client, and it
has paid off more than any framework.

## Your model is only as good as the query that feeds it

Every label, every feature, every evaluation slice starts as a query. If
you cannot write it, you are asking someone else to, and the translation
loses information. If you can, you control the most important part of the
pipeline: what the model is actually allowed to learn from.

## SQL is where the data tells the truth

Before any training run, I look at the raw data myself: distributions,
nulls, duplicates, a few hand-picked rows. SQL is the fastest way to ask
questions and get answers without waiting for anyone. The habits of looking
first and trusting later have caught more problems than every unit test I
have written.

## Explaining the work in SQL makes it real

A metric definition written in SQL is a fact. A metric definition written
in a slide is a wish. When I hand the product team a query that computes the
metric, we stop arguing about what we meant and start arguing about what the
number says, which is the argument you want to have.

## Debugging production means reading the data

Model misbehaving? The first move is not the model. It is the data: what
changed, what is missing, what arrived broken. SQL is the language of that
investigation, and being fluent in it means the difference between an hour
and a day.

## Learn the boring parts first

Window functions, joins with duplicates, date handling, null semantics,
explain plans. None of it is glamorous, all of it is load-bearing. This is
the part of ML that never appears in courses and always appears in
postmortems.

## A practical path if you are starting now

Do not take a course. Take a real problem: a metric that needs
computing, a table that needs exploring, a label set that needs
building. Write the queries, fight the nulls, and read the explain
plans when things get slow. The first week will be humbling; the first
month will change how you see every pipeline.

Then add the two habits that pay forever: look at raw data before
trusting any summary, and write your metric definitions as queries
that anyone can run. These are not SQL skills so much as truth
skills, and SQL happens to be where they are practised. Once you have
them, frameworks come and go without moving your foundations.

If you are early in your ML career and deciding what to study next, close
the paper list and open a SQL workbook. Your future self, three production
incidents deep, will thank you.
