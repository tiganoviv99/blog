My first ML role was at a consulting company, building models for enterprise
clients across different industries. I went in expecting to learn models.
I came out having learned something more useful: how to sell the boring
parts.

## Clients do not buy models, they buy outcomes

Nobody signs a contract for a gradient-boosted ensemble. They sign for
"fewer chargebacks", "faster inventory turns", "fewer support tickets". The
engineers who thrive in consulting are the ones who can connect the model
they are building to the number the client actually cares about.

This habit stayed with me long after: before starting any ML work, write
down which business metric it is supposed to move and how you will know.
If you cannot, the work is not ready to start.

## Scoping is where projects are won or lost

A vague request like "use AI to improve our process" is a trap. Good scoping
turns it into a concrete question with a success criterion, a timeline, and
an explicit list of things that are out of scope. I learned to write that
list down and get it agreed, because scope creep is the default state of
client projects.

The same discipline applies inside product companies, where the "client" is a
product manager with a deadline.

## The boring deliverable is the real deliverable

Clients love a demo. But what they keep using is the pipeline, the
documentation, the evaluation report, the handover notes. The demo decays;
the plumbing stays. I learned to spend real time on the parts nobody
claps for, because those are the parts that determine whether the project
survives the consultant's departure.

## Communication beats cleverness

A consultant's most expensive resource is the meeting. A clear one-page
summary that a non-technical stakeholder can act on is worth more than a
brilliant architecture slide. I practised writing those summaries until they
became a reflex, and it paid off in every role since.

## What I took with me

Consulting compressed years of exposure into months: different stacks,
different data maturity, different definitions of done. The models blurred
together. The skills that compounded were scoping, writing, and connecting
technical work to business outcomes.

## Estimates are honesty tests

Consulting also taught me to treat estimates as a form of communication.
A confident estimate with hidden assumptions is worse than a rough estimate
with assumptions written down. I started writing estimates as "X weeks,
assuming A and B; plus C weeks if D happens". Clients rarely remember the
precision. They remember whether reality matched what you said.

Inside product companies the same skill applies with different vocabulary:
roadmaps, commitments, quarterly planning. The engineers who estimate
honestly get trusted with bigger things. The ones who estimate to please get
managed. Both outcomes compound. Pick the first one, and start small: say
what you are assuming, out loud, every time. That habit alone is worth a
year of consulting.

If you are early in your ML career and have a chance to do consulting-style
work, take it. You will learn less about exotic architectures and far more
about why projects actually succeed.
