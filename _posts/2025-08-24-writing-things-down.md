I have made the case for writing before, and I will keep making it,
because it is the single habit with the best ratio of effort to payoff
in this entire field. This is the longer version, for the day you
actually need it.

## Memory is a lossy format

Every project ends with lessons. Almost all of them are forgotten
within months, because the brain prioritises the next deadline over the
last retrospective. Writing is the only reliable compression format I
have found: cheap to produce, cheap to keep, invaluable later.

## Writing is thinking in public

Half the value of a written note is not the note; it is the thinking
that produced it. Ideas that feel clear in the head collapse on the
page, and that collapse is information. I have killed more bad ideas
while writing them down than in any meeting.

## The compounding is real

One postmortem is trivia. Fifty postmortems are a pattern library.
A year of written decisions is an institutional memory that survives
anyone's departure. The blog posts, the runbooks, the design docs:
each is small, and together they compound into reputation and clarity.

## You don't need a platform

A private notebook, a team wiki, a blog: the medium does not matter.
The cadence does. Ten minutes after a project ends, while the details
are warm, write the three things you would tell the next person. That
note, written on time, is worth more than a polished document written
months later from memory.

## The habit is the asset

Not the blog. Not the audience. The habit of converting experience
into words is the asset, and it pays in better decisions, faster
onboarding, and a body of work that speaks for you when you are not in
the room.

## What to write when you have nothing to say

Most people abandon writing because they wait for something worth
saying. The trick is that almost nothing feels worth saying until it is
written. The bar is low and should stay low: three sentences about a
bug you fixed, a decision you made, a meeting that changed your mind.
Small notes are not failed essays; they are the raw material that essays
come from, and a year of small notes reads like a career in review.

I keep a file per project and append freely, without editing. The
editing happens later, if the note grows into something public. The
file itself never pressures anyone. That is the whole system, and it
works because it is too small to fail.

Start ugly. Start short. Start today. The version of you that reads it
in a year will not care how it looked; they will care that it exists.
