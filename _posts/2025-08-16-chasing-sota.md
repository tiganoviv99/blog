title: "Chasing the state of the art is a career tax"

Every week there is a new model, a new paper, a new technique that will
supposedly change everything. Most of it will not survive the quarter,
and none of it will ship your feature for you. Chasing the state of the
art is how engineers buy anxiety and call it professional development.

## The frontier moves faster than your project

By the time you have read the paper, reproduced the benchmark, and
integrated the idea, the frontier has moved twice. That is fine for
researchers; it is a tax for product engineers. The job is not to be at
the frontier. The job is to pick the point on the frontier that solves
the problem at an acceptable cost.

## Proven boring beats unproven exciting

In production, the value of a technique is not its novelty; it is its
reliability. A five-year-old method that is well understood, well
tooled, and well staffed beats a three-week-old method that is none of
those. The boring choice is often the brave choice, because it forgoes
the excitement for the outcome.

## The portfolio view

Treat your skills like a portfolio: a large position in fundamentals,
a small position in promising new things. The fundamentals compound
forever: data, evaluation, systems, judgment. The new things have a
high failure rate and a real payoff when one hits. The portfolio keeps
you employable through any hype cycle.

## Curiosity without commitment

There is a healthy middle path: read broadly, track what is happening,
and let the field surprise you. Then wait. If a technique is still
alive in six months, and it maps to a real problem you have, that is
the time to invest. The wait costs nothing and filters out most of the
noise.

## What actually compounds

Judgment, shipped systems, written lessons, trusted colleagues. None of
them require the latest model. All of them outlast it.

## The one exception

There is one case where chasing the frontier is rational: when the new
technique changes the cost of what was previously impossible. A model
that turns a six-month labelling project into a weekend job is not hype;
it is a price change, and price changes deserve attention. The question
is always the same: does this new thing make a problem cheaper, or does
it just make a conversation more interesting? Answer that honestly, and
you will know whether to chase.

You do not owe the frontier your attention. You owe your users a
working system and yourself a sustainable career. Spend accordingly.

The field rewards the patient more than the fast, and it always has.
The engineers who matter in ten years are not the ones who chased
every release; they are the ones who kept shipping, kept learning, and
let the hype spend itself against their calm. That is a strategy you
can start applying this afternoon, and it costs nothing.
