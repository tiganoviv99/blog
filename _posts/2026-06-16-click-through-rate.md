There is a metric that always goes up when you make thumbnails bigger, titles
louder, and recommendations more predictable. It is called click-through
rate, and teams keep celebrating it while the business metric next to it goes
nowhere.

## Clicks are not value

A click is a hypothesis the user is testing, not a purchase they made. You
can raise click-through rate by showing things people will click out of
curiosity and abandon. Congratulations: you have optimised disappointment.

The metric that survived our experience was value per session: revenue,
conversion, repeat usage. Clicks stayed as a diagnostic, never as a goal.

## The bait-and-switch detection

If a model wins on clicks but loses on conversion, it is bait. If it wins on
clicks and conversion but loses on repeat visits, it is a hangover: people
clicked, bought, and felt tricked. Both patterns are common, and both are
invisible if the dashboard ends at click-through.

## Engagement can be manufactured

Same story with scroll depth, time on page, and session length. A feed that
shows twenty half-relevant items manufactures scrolls. A feed that shows
five good ones manufactures purchases. Optimising engagement measures is
like optimising for how long someone stays lost in your store.

## The discipline that helped

We treated any engagement metric as a guardrail with a floor, not a target.
The target was always a value metric. Experiments had to state their expected
effect on both, and an engagement win with a value loss was counted as a
loss. This rule alone changed the kind of models we built: fewer tricks,
more substance.

## The metric diet

If your team's dashboard has more engagement metrics than value
metrics, the dashboard is the problem. We put every metric through a
simple test: if this number moved up and the business number moved
down, would we still be proud? If the answer is yes, the metric is a
drug and belongs in diagnostics, not on the trophy shelf.

The diet was uncomfortable, because engagement numbers are flattering
and value numbers are stubborn. But within a quarter, the
conversations changed: experiments were proposed in terms of revenue
and conversion first, and the engagement tricks quietly stopped being
proposed. Metrics shape behaviour more reliably than any process, and
the diet shaped ours in the right direction.

What to do on Monday

Open your experiment dashboard and find the metric everyone brags about.
Then find the number that actually pays the bills. If the first is not
causally downstream of the second, you have found the drug. Detox slowly:
promote the value metric, demote the rest, and watch the roadmap get more
honest.
